Solar Cells: Working Principle, Properties, Characteristics and Applications

Explore the physics of photovoltaic energy conversion, the properties of semiconductor materials, electrical performance parameters, interactive solar-cell graphs and the role of solar energy in a sustainable future.

1. Importance of Solar Cells

Solar cells are essential components of modern renewable-energy systems. They convert solar radiation directly into electricity without combustion during operation. Photovoltaic technology can contribute to energy security, lower operational emissions and electrification in locations connected or not connected to an electricity grid.

Renewable energy

Sunlight is a naturally replenished energy resource. Photovoltaic systems help diversify the electricity supply.

Environmental benefits

Electricity generation requires no fuel combustion at the panel. Manufacturing, transport and end-of-life impacts must also be considered in a complete life-cycle assessment.

Energy independence

Rooftop systems and solar farms can reduce dependence on imported fuels and diversify local energy production.

Remote applications

Solar systems can power sensors, telecommunications, lighting, irrigation and equipment in remote areas.

Solar electricity is variable because irradiance changes with time, weather, season and shading. Batteries, grid interconnection and complementary energy sources can help balance supply and demand.

2. Definition of a Solar Cell

A solar cell, also called a photovoltaic cell, is a semiconductor device that converts incident light into electrical energy through the photovoltaic effect.

A solar cell absorbs photons and generates mobile charge carriers. An internal electric field or selective electrical contacts help separate and collect electrons and holes. When the contacts are connected through an external circuit, electrical current can flow and deliver power to a load.

Energy-conversion sequence: sunlight → photon absorption → electron–hole pair generation → charge separation → current through an external circuit.

3. Physical Principle and Operation

3.1 Photon absorption

Light consists of photons carrying energy. The energy of a photon is related to its frequency and wavelength by:

E = hν = hc / λ

Here, h is Planck's constant, ν is frequency, c is the speed of light and λ is wavelength. In a semiconductor, photons with sufficient energy can excite electrons into higher-energy states, leaving holes behind.

3.2 Electron–hole pair generation

In a simplified band model, a photon can generate an electron–hole pair when its energy is at least comparable to the semiconductor band gap, Eg. Photons with lower energy generally cannot generate carriers across that gap.

Photon energy ≥ semiconductor band-gap energy

3.3 Charge separation

A p–n junction or another charge-selective structure helps separate electrons and holes. The internal electric field and interfaces direct carriers toward their appropriate contacts, reducing the chance that they recombine before collection.

3.4 Electrical power generation

When a load connects the two contacts, the cell supplies current at a voltage. The useful electrical power is the product of voltage and current.

P = V × I

3.5 Recombination and energy losses

Not every incident photon produces a collected carrier. Reflection, transmission, thermalization, recombination and resistive losses reduce conversion efficiency. Materials engineering and improved interfaces can reduce some of these losses.

4. Solar-Cell Structure and Materials

A conventional solar cell can contain a light-absorbing semiconductor, a junction or selective contacts, a front contact, a rear contact and optical coatings. At module level, protective glass, encapsulation and interconnections protect and combine cells.

ComponentFunction
Absorber layerAbsorbs photons and generates charge carriers.
Junction / selective contactsSeparate and extract electrons and holes.
Front electrodeCollects current while allowing light to enter.
Anti-reflection coatingReduces reflection losses.
Rear electrodeProvides the second electrical contact.
EncapsulationProtects module components from environmental exposure.

Common absorber materials

  • Crystalline silicon.
  • Cadmium telluride (CdTe).
  • Copper indium gallium selenide (CIGS).
  • Metal-halide perovskites.
  • Organic semiconductor materials.
  • Multijunction combinations of different absorbers.

5. Important Physical Properties

Band gap

Determines the minimum photon energy needed for excitation and strongly influences the voltage and spectral response.

Absorption coefficient

Describes how strongly a material absorbs light at a particular wavelength.

Carrier mobility

Describes how readily electrons or holes move under an electric field.

Carrier lifetime

Indicates how long generated carriers survive before recombination.

Defect density

Defects can provide recombination pathways and reduce carrier collection.

Stability

Thermal, chemical and environmental stability affect device reliability and service life.

The best absorber is not determined by one property alone. Researchers must consider band gap, optical absorption, carrier transport, recombination, contacts, stability, manufacturing cost, material availability and environmental impact together.

6. Electrical Characteristics of Solar Cells

6.1 Short-circuit current (Isc)

The short-circuit current is the current delivered when the terminal voltage is zero. It depends strongly on illumination, active area, optical absorption and carrier collection.

6.2 Open-circuit voltage (Voc)

The open-circuit voltage is measured when the external current is zero. It depends on the semiconductor, recombination, temperature and illumination.

6.3 Maximum power point

The maximum power point is the operating point where the product of current and voltage is greatest.

Pmax = Vmp × Imp

6.4 Fill factor

The fill factor measures the quality or rectangularity of the current–voltage curve.

FF = Pmax / (Voc × Isc)

A fill factor closer to 1 indicates that the maximum output power is closer to the product of open-circuit voltage and short-circuit current. Resistive losses and recombination can reduce FF.

6.5 Power-conversion efficiency

η = Pmax / Pin × 100%

Pin is the optical power incident on the device. Combining the definitions gives:

η = (Voc × Isc × FF) / Pin × 100%

For current density Jsc, use incident irradiance and power density consistently.

For common terrestrial photovoltaic measurements, standard test conditions typically use 1000 W/m² irradiance, an AM1.5 reference spectrum and a cell temperature of 25 °C. Always state measurement conditions when comparing solar cells.

6.6 Other characterization parameters

  • External and internal quantum efficiency.
  • Series resistance and shunt resistance.
  • Temperature coefficients.
  • Spectral response.
  • Dark and illuminated current–voltage measurements.

7. Interactive Solar-Cell Characteristics

The following graphs are generated from an illustrative mathematical model. They are educational examples, not experimental data for a specific solar-cell technology.

Maximum power: —

Current versus voltage (I–V)

At zero voltage, the current is approximately Isc. As voltage approaches Voc, the current falls toward zero. The bend or knee indicates the region where the cell can deliver its maximum power.

Power versus voltage (P–V)

The P–V curve is calculated using P = V × I. Its peak corresponds to the maximum power point, which is important for maximum-power point tracking in practical photovoltaic systems.

Worked numerical example

Suppose a hypothetical cell has Voc = 0.65 V, Isc = 8.0 A and FF = 0.75. Its estimated maximum power is:

Pmax = 0.65 × 8.0 × 0.75 = 3.90 W

If the incident optical power is 20 W, the efficiency is 3.90 / 20 × 100% = 19.5%. These figures are illustrative only.

8. Applications of Solar Cells

Residential

Rooftop electricity generation for homes.

Industrial

Commercial roofs and large photovoltaic plants.

Agriculture

Irrigation pumps, water management and sensors.

Remote systems

Telecommunications, lighting and standalone equipment.

Space technology

Electrical power for satellites and spacecraft.

Emerging research

Tandem photovoltaics, indoor cells and solar-powered hydrogen.

9. Advantages and Limitations

Advantages

  • No fuel combustion during generation.
  • Modular and scalable design.
  • Suitable for grid-connected or off-grid systems.
  • Can reduce purchased electricity.
  • Pairs with battery storage.

Limitations

  • Output varies with sunlight and weather.
  • Shading can reduce performance.
  • Storage and power electronics may add cost.
  • Manufacturing requires materials and energy.
  • Recycling and end-of-life management are important.
